## 1. Learning outcomes and editorial argument

The reader should be able to (a) explain why 323 BCE did not produce three instantly settled successor kingdoms, (b) identify how Ptolemaic, Seleucid and Antigonid power depended on revenue, soldiers, elites and cities, (c) distinguish a city's surviving civic institutions from its effective diplomatic or military autonomy, (d) compare the self-presentation of royal letters and civic decrees with administrative papyri, (e) recognize multiple populations and unequal participation rather than a uniform Greek replacement of local cultures, and (f) assess the consequences of Roman intervention without treating the period as mere prelude.

**Investigative question:** What happened to cities and inhabitants when different kings claimed rule over territories conquered by Alexander? The opening is a concrete third-century Egyptian tax-related Zenon papyrus; a second dossier follows Magnesia on the Maeander obtaining responses in 208/7 BCE. These are **different contexts and generations**, explicitly separated. Neither supplies a universal experience.

### Access/provenance notes

- The Magnesia dossier is unusually well grounded in directly accessible **ancient texts in translation** and Ceccarelli's abstract. The source is not neutral: an inscribed selection of documents also constructs civic prestige.
- The Zenon photograph's short object description is **not itself an edited papyrus text**. Therefore the lesson does not quote its original wording, prescribe a specific amount, or reconstruct an individual debtor.
- Polybius and later narrative histories are useful for the broader period, but **no unverified chapter or verbatim passage has been credited to them** in this version. The Roman transition rests on high-level scholarship, pending specialist review of individual chronology.
- Non-Greek and non-elite experiences are disproportionately underdocumented. Archaeology and demotic documents help but do not erase archival bias.

## 5. Historical uncertainty, possible disputes and exclusions

1. **Succession:** 323 BCE marks a contested situation, not an immediate permanent tripartition. The timing and applicability of specific royal titles in each region should be independently rechecked before approval.
2. **Autonomy and asylia:** diplomatic recognition, theoretical civic freedom, practical garrison control and ability to refuse tribute are **distinct variables**. The Magnesian inscriptions show petitions/responses; do not assume exact garrison conditions there.
3. **Magnesia inscription:** AIO identifies the Magnesia Agora wall copy and possible abridgement of an Athenian decree; the exact original Athens inscription setting is uncertain.
4. **Seleucid city foundations:** foundation dates, demographic relocation, prior settlements and changing regions differ; Gerardin's 200–100 BCE scope cannot prove all third-century cases.
5. **Taxes, land and papyri:** Zenon letters are a selected managerial archive, not a representative sample of all cultivators. Generalized land-ownership formulas are hazardous.
6. **Bilingualism:** S.6080 supports coexistence of written genres around a priestly archive, **not** universal bilingualism or cultural equality.
7. **Numismatics:** Commons file label is not a complete scholarly attribution; do not present exact coin mint, issuing date or named royal physiognomy as established.
8. **Rome:** changing leverage preceded and followed 168 BCE; city constitutions and later kingdoms persisted in varied forms. This lesson deliberately stops short of a Roman conquest chronology.
9. **Evidence quality:** editorial specialist must verify primary editions, disputed interpretations and modern Greek renderings before Approved. Ancient text's own claims remain explicitly separated from independent corroboration.
