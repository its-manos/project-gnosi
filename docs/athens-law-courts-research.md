## Editorial position, title, objective
- Category: Ιστορία. Learning path: Αρχαία Ελλάδα, 30 planned lessons in five chapters. Chapter III: Ο κόσμος των πόλεων.
- Working title: «Νόμοι, δικαστήρια και κλήρωση στην Αθήνα».
- **Final title:** «Αθήνα: από την καταγγελία στην ψήφο». The title prioritizes a specific interpretive journey (claim → procedure → judgement) rather than listing institutions. It also separates the story from Lesson 15's question of voting rights in the assembly.
- Objective: explain how a claim or public charge could reach an Athenian court, how the citizen jury was chosen and voted, how litigants made and challenged persuasive claims, how procedures changed, and who enjoyed unequal access. Never treat fifth- and fourth-century systems as timeless or ancient jurors as modern professional judges.
- Prose: approximately **1,305 Greek words** including intro, reading estimate **11 minutes including six reasoning breaks**; six sections, 28 stable paragraphs `courts-01`…`courts-28`. This duration is an estimate, not a stopwatch/device finding.

## Narrative arc and evidence map

| Paragraphs | Evidence and scope | Interpretative caution |
|---|---|---|
| `courts-01`–`05` | Laws, decrees, verdicts; IG I³ 104, republished 409/408 BCE (S1–S3); law/decree changes after 403/402 BCE (S4–S5) | The re-inscribed homicide law does not date the surviving stone to Draco's lifetime; the old law's text is damaged; precise fourth-century nomothesia procedure disputed |
| `courts-06`–`10` | Private `dikē` and public `graphē`, magistrates, arbitration, special homicide procedures and maritime commerce claims (S6–S8) | No universal state prosecution service; not a simple modern civil/criminal divide; some noncitizens' commercial legal standing is context specific |
| `courts-11`–`15` | Late-fourth-century `Athenaion Politeia` (30+ citizen juror eligibility and exclusions, pay, allocation), bronze juror identifiers and stone `klērotērion` (S6, S9–S10) | Physical grooves are not a full instruction manual; avoid backdating fourth-century allocation to Solon's Athens; distinguish `pinakia` from ballots |
| `courts-16`–`20` | Forensic speeches and logographers; witnesses, written documents, time-limited speeches/klepsydra; Lysias, Demosthenes, oratorical evidence (S6, S7, S11–S12) | One party's preserved assertion is not independently established fact; source preservation is selective; testimony and legal standing varied by context |
| `courts-21`–`24` | Bronze juror ballots, pierced and solid stems, separate voting and tally, ostracism distinction, confidence and control (S6, S13) | Court ballots are not ostraka; no universal jury size or modern system of appeals/fully reasoned judgments asserted |
| `courts-25`–`28` | Eligibility barriers, unequal access/credibility, satire of juries in Aristophanes' *Wasps* (422 BCE), synthesis of sources (S7, S11, S14) | Popular participation is not universal equality; *Wasps* is satire and Plato a critic, not transparent stenography |

**Institutions and archaeology:** the Ancient Agora is an archaeological context with many phases; not every standing structure represents one fifth-century trial court. The lesson privileges dated objects over architectural speculation.

**Chronology:** Draconian laws are conventionally linked with late seventh century, Solon with early sixth, the inscribed homicide-law republication with 409/408, and lawmaking alterations after 403/402; the *Athenaion Politeia* (attributed to Aristotle's school or Aristotle) describes mechanisms chiefly in the late fourth century. The fifth-century evidence must not silently inherit all later technical details.

**Scholarly alternatives:** scholars differ about exact phases and composition of the post-403/402 nomothesia process, about the reconstruction and integrity of inserted speeches and documentary sections, and about forensic rhetoric as formal evidence vs broad social performance. The article avoids a unique unchallenged reconstruction.

**Social access:** judicial standing is differentiated: adult male citizens and jurors, women of citizen families involved via male representatives in many suits, metics and noncitizen maritime litigants under certain procedures, and enslaved people with sharply restricted positions in legal testimony. This is not a second broad lesson on eligibility to vote in the assembly.

## Scholarly and primary source ledger
Sources consulted as evidence **for the lesson's claims**, not as a substitute for checking particular contexts and dates. Some URLs are publisher pages/abstracts; reader should not assume full-text access.

- **S1.** *Drakon’s Homicide Law (IG I³ 104)*. Aleshire Center for the Study of Greek Epigraphy, University of California, Berkeley. Dated public inscription re-publication 409/408 BCE. https://aleshire.berkeley.edu/inscriptions/1923
- **S2.** Michael Gagarin, *Writing Greek Law*, Cambridge University Press (2008), chapter “Why Draco Wrote his Homicide Law.” https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/writing-greek-law/why-draco-wrote-his-homicide-law/75C620B643690D8FDEE7ECAD03B9114E
- **S3.** Edward M. Harris and Mirko Canevaro, “Towards a New Text of Draco’s Law on Homicide,” *Revue des Études Grecques*, 136 (2023). Challenges concerning reconstruction of damaged text. https://www.persee.fr/doc/reg_0035-2039_2023_num_136_1_8758
- **S4.** Mirko Canevaro, “Nomothesia in Classical Athens: What Sources Should We Believe?”, *Classical Quarterly* 63/1 (2013). https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/nomothesia-in-classical-athens-what-sources-should-we-believe/
- **S5.** Douglas M. MacDowell, “Law-making at Athens in the Fourth Century B.C.”, *Journal of Hellenic Studies* 95 (1975), pp. 62–74. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-hellenic-studies/article/abs/lawmaking-at-athens-in-the-fourth-century-bc/DCF9659168D6EF3767A57404386C7D14
- **S6.** *Athenaion Politeia* / *The Athenian Constitution*, especially chapters 56–59 and 62–69, late 4th-century Aristotelian tradition; translation F. G. Kenyon, MIT Internet Classics Archive. Ancient literary evidence, not proof that every procedure began in 6th century BCE. https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/athenian_const.3.3.html
- **S7.** “Building Legal Order in Ancient Athens,” *Journal of Legal Analysis* 7/2 (2015), Oxford Academic, pp. 291ff. https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/7/2/291/1751160
- **S8.** “Legislation (nomothesia)”, *Oxford Classical Dictionary*, Oxford University Press. https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550525087
- **S9.** Robert Sing, “The Rates of Jury Pay and Assembly Pay in Fourth-Century Athens,” *Classical Quarterly* (2021), pp. 119–134. Supports period-sensitive pay discussions; do not extrapolate into archaic Athens.
- **S10.** *Kleroterion*, archaeological finds and documented museum photographs, American School of Classical Studies at Athens / Museum of the Ancient Agora. Image/object pages listed below.
- **S11.** Adriaan Lanni, “Evidence: Ancient Athens,” *Oxford International Encyclopedia of Legal History* (2009).
- **S12.** Adriaan Lanni, “Relevance in Athenian Courts,” *Cambridge Companion to Ancient Greek Law* (2005).
- **S13.** Bronze judicial ballots from Ancient Agora Museum; Commons photography and museum labels in file below.
- **S14.** Aristophanes, *Wasps* (first produced 422 BCE), theatrical satire of juror service; not an ordinary court transcript.
