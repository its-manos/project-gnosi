## Historical claim ledger

| Central claim | Direct evidence | Interpretation / limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Hoplite arms include a large round shield, spear and variable bronze protection | Surviving armor; Met *Warfare in Ancient Greece* (2000); Ministry of Culture's exhibition; Commons photograph with item labels | Objects in the photo do **not** form an archaeologically unified panoply; wooden, leather and textile elements preserve unevenly |
| Double-grip shield occurs around 700 BCE | Viggiano & van Wees (2013), pp. 57–73, published abstract | An equipment innovation does not establish a universal drill or same-year political change |
| Corinthian helmets and other protective gear varied | Met helmet 20.51.1; British Museum helmet 1873,0910.1, approx. 500 BCE | Form-name alone cannot identify the provenance, allegiance or identity of all users |
| Close-group fighting was a meaningful archaic cultural theme | Corinthian Chigi olpe c. 650–640 BCE; Hurwit (2002) | The painted composition is **not** a tactical photograph; portrayal is embedded in a wider sequence of myth and social scenes |
| Standing fast and support by other troops mattered | Tyrtaeus fr. 11; Romney (2014) | Poetry urges conduct and is not direct evidence for exact depth/width of a particular phalanx |
| Formation cohesion and shield cover were consequential | Thucydides 5.70–71 on 418 BCE | An author's account of one campaign cannot be projected backward as the universal rule from 700 BCE |
| Terrain and lightweight arms could limit hoplites | Xenophon, *Anabasis* 3.4.19–29, events after 401 BCE | Later context; evidence of adaptive practice rather than every conflict being decided by heavy infantry |
| Service and political rights were related differently across cities | Krentz (2007); comparative political and military scholarship | No deterministic chain equipment → one formation → democracy, nor one universal Spartan training regime |
| Evolution of phalanx remains disputed | Snodgrass (2006), Hanson (2013), van Wees (2000), Krentz (2002, 2007) | Distinguish chronology of artifacts, represented tactics, actual practice, ritual-war ideology and institutional reforms |

**Disagreement handled fairly:** Hanson defends a broad polis/hoplite interpretation; van Wees cautions against a rapid equipment-to-uniform-formation inference; Snodgrass weighs actual surviving defensive equipment against reconstructed literary tactics; Krentz challenges a simple early development of ritualized hoplite war and its political implications. These positions cannot be collapsed into a proven date for a single “revolution.” The Chigi image proves a contemporary representation of group action; it does not settle the debate.

## Geographic and temporal precision

- **Archaic:** transition around the end of the eighth and seventh centuries BCE; distinctive shield commonly dated near 700 BCE; the Chigi olpe is Corinthian and dated c. 650–640 BCE. The olpe was found in the Veii region of Italy and is held in Rome. Do not call the painted warriors eyewitnesses of a named real battle.
- **Classical:** the British Museum bronze helmet is approximately 500 BCE; the cited Thucydidean narrative addresses a battle in **418 BCE**. The Persian Wars are not narrated here.
- **Early fourth century:** Xenophon's *Anabasis* recounts events beginning after **401 BCE**; does not determine the seventh-century formation.
- **Museum-display geography:** the 2021 Athens exhibit includes finds from Lamia, Hermione/Argolis, Olympia, Acropolis of Athens and Mount Lykaion/Arcadia. These labels are individual provenances. The photo does not create a new findspot for those ancient objects.
- **Military-social comparators:** Sparta, Athens, Argos, Corinth and Boeotia appear only to establish variation, not to retell prior political institutions or yet-unwritten war chronology.
