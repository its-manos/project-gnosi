## Objective and editorial decisions
Explain how Achaemenid governance and western Anatolian/Greek political decisions led into the Ionian Revolt, the 490 expedition and Xerxes' 480–479 campaigns. Separate event chronology, strategic/logistical hypotheses, source genre and later commemoration. Avoid national binaries, unsupported numbers, invented Persian council dialogue and an Athens-only narrative. The improved title names three interacting realities: empire, cities and historically constrained choices. The original title's "two worlds" risked implying two unified civilizations.

## Scholarly uncertainty and common traps
- No intact, direct Achaemenid campaign chronicle equivalent to Herodotus survives; Persian administrative and royal sources support different claims.
- Ionian Revolt details are highly dependent on Herodotus, not all archaeologically cross-checked. The cycle of submission and revolt must not be turned into modern nationalism.
- Darius' and Xerxes' intentions, invasion scale, exact numbers and battle tactical reconstructions are contested; the prose intentionally avoids artificial precision and invented quotations.
- Herodotus' precise claim that Plataea and Mycale occurred on the same day is treated as a literary claim, not independently confirmed chronology.
- The archaeological context of commemorative graves and the later inscription of a public dedication are not verbatim battle reports.
- Memorialized names omit rowers, workers, women, noncitizens, people forced into service and anonymous dead. Avoid inferring equal enfranchisement from wartime participation.
- Geography is given by real regional routes and constraints, not one deceptively exact 'battle map'. Today's Salamis coastline is not equivalent to an ancient navigational chart.
- The empire did not dissolve in 479 BCE and the Delian League / imperial transformation belongs entirely to Lesson 20.
